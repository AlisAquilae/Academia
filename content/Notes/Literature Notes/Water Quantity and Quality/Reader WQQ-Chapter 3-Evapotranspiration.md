# 3.1 Evapotranspiration and the earth's water and energy cycles
Strictly speaking, evaporation (verdamping) is the phase transition between the liquid and the gaseous phase. In hydrological and climate science however, the evaporation from the land surface is usually referred to as [[Evapotranspiration]] (a combination of evaporation and transpiration), to highlight the fact that water can be evaporated from different sources. 
- When water is being evaporated from the soil, open water or from rainfall that is intercepted by canopy, this is simply referred to as evaporation. 
- When the evaporating water surface is located within leave's stomata, it is referred to as transpiration to indicate the strong biophysical control over the process (although physically there is no difference between evaporation and transpiration)

>[!Info]
>In practice, evapotranspiration might also include [[Sublimation]] (direct phase transition between the solid and gaseous phase).


The opposite process to evaporation, the phase transition between the gaseous and liquid phase, is called condensation and takes place during dew formation.

Evapotranspiration takes place whenever a liquid phase is in contact with air that is not saturated with water. The driving force behind evapotranspiration is the difference between vapour pressure at saturation ($e^* = f(T)$) and the actual water vapour pressure ($e$) of the near-surface air, often referred to as [[Vapour Pressure Deficit]] (VPD):

$$
VPD = e^* (T_{\text{air}}) - e
$$

>[!Note]
>Note that the pressure is usually expressed in Pa but for convenience often hPa, kPa or mbar are used

The partial pressure of water vapour $e$ is independent of the air pressure. The humidity deficit is often expressed in terms of the [[Specific Humidity]] $q$ (the ratio between the mass of water vapour and the mass of dry air), which has the advantage that it does not change with air density

$$
\Delta q = q^* (T) - q
$$

in which $q^* (T)$ is the value of $q$ at saturation

The saturated vapour pressure of air $e^*$ increases almost exponentially with temperature, through the relationship known as the [[Clausius-Clapeyron Equation|Clausius-Clapeyron Relation]].
