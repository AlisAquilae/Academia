# 2.1 Introduction
Geologically speaking, natural [[Delta|Deltas]] are very dynamic landscapes. The delta rivers continuously deposit sediments on the delta plain, while the channels may laterally migrate across the plain by bank erosion and deposition of lateral bars. From time to time, the delta rivers more or less suddenly shift to a completely new position on the delta plain, a process which is called [[Avulsion]]. At and near the river mouth new land is formed, which leads to channel extension. Longshore [[Coastal Sediment Transport|marine transport of sediments]] and [[Aeolian Sediment Transport|aeolian processes]] facilitate formation of beach ridges. Salt marshes in the sheltered [[Lagoon|Lagoons]] or other parts of the delta coast gradually grow upward by trapping fine sediments. On the other hand, waves may attack and erode beach ridges and salt marshes, and tidal channel systems may erosively expand inland. Additionally, parts of the delta plain that receive little sediment gradually drown because of slow subsidence, potentially aided by sea-level rise. Drowning is followed by intensification of marine erosion.

The schematic of delta dynamics above describes the overlapping growth and decline of delta lobes, a result of constructive and destructive forces that may continuously change in space and time. This can be thought of as the "Delta Cycle":
- The constructive forces are [[Fluvial Sediment Transport|fluvial]] sediment influx and deposition (or redeposition) by fluvial, marine and aeolian processes
- The destructive forces are marine erosion by waves and tidal currents, and subsidence and sea-level rise

Central to the constructive forces of delta evolution is the process of [[Avulsion]]. Avulsion determines the location of a river channel and its mouth, which importantly determines which part of the delta receives enough sediment to grow. Understanding the causes and mechanisms of avulsion is the key to understanding delta evolution on the millennia timescale. 

The destructive forces of delta evolution are strongly influenced by relative land-level lowering, which on its turn is determined by sea-level change and subsidence. Relative land-level lowering, which in itself already causes degradation of the sub-aerial delta, makes the delta vulnerable for the destructive marine forces of wave and tidal current erosion.

Before we can discuss the constructive and destructive forces of delta formation in depth, some general principles of delta evolution need to be outlined.

# 2.2 Base-Level Changes and Delta Evolution
## 2.2.1 The Concept of Base-Level
Over the course of geological time, any river will tend to develop a smooth and concave gradient line by local erosion and deposition. In the upper catchment there is much potential for erosion, whereas near its mouth the erosive potential is very limited and deposition will prevail. The lowest level in a river gradient line is sea level, because rivers cannot erode (very far) below this level. Of course in channels rivers can erode slightly below sea level, but at the same time they will construct wide floodplains by overbank deposition that are at or slightly above sea level. For this reason, sea level is called the **Base Level** if the river gradient line.

Now consider that sea level (base level) rises. Then the river gradient line has to adjust to a new base-level position. Because the lower part of the gradient line becomes very gentle, the sediment transport capacity will decrease and deposition will increase and a new gradient line at a higher position will be established. The difference between the old and the new base level is called the [[Accommodation Space]], i.e. this is the space that can be filled up with sediments, provided that these are sufficiently available. If sea level drops the erosive potential in the lower part of the gradient line increases, because a higher gradient to sea level is created. This leads to establishment of a new gradient line by incision of the lower part.

These principles imply that a thick intact delta sediment body can only form under conditions of a rising or stable sea level. If sea level drops the river will continuously erode its own delta deposits. On the other hand one can imagine that if sea level rises very fast the delta surface will drown, because the adjustment of the river gradient line cannot keep pace with the rising base level. In other words, there is insufficient sediment input to fill the accommodation space.
