In a fluvial dominated deltaic systems, sediment is deposited near the river mouth and mouth bars create channel bifurcations. However, the diffusive character of wave energy and wave refraction acts to erode mouth bars. Sand is subsequently transported along the beach by wave-generated alongshore currents, resulting in relatively smooth shorelines. Finer grained material from deltas remains in suspension by wave energy and is commonly transported offshore away from the beach. Therefore, it is often assumed that mostly coarse grained sediments contribute to beach progradation and wave-dominated delta formation. The fine-grained sediment is then eventually deposited as deltaic foreset below the shoreface where the delta eventually can prograde over. 

In this chapter, first, a general description of the beach is given. Secondly, the generation of the longshore current and sediment transport is discussed. Thirdly, a concept to model shoreline evolution is discussed (called the 1-line concept). Thereafter, it is discussed how the longshore sediment transport causes wave-influenced deltas to have a cuspate shape. Finally, a method to quantitatively determine the wave dominance of a delta is discussed.

# 7.1 General Description of Beaches
Beaches consist of unconsolidated material transported by waves and currents. As you learned earlier, waves induce currents (their orbital motion) that can affect the sea bed if the wave is sufficiently long or the water is sufficient shallow. Sediment grain size generally follows this trend, and tends to be finer away from the beach towards the continental shelf where the orbital currents are smaller. Generally, coarse material is found landward of the bars and finer material on the seaward side.

![[Pasted image 20241123144433.png]]

The beach profile refers to the cross-section from the dunes to the inner continental shelf where waves (and currents) affect the sea bed, and where the sea bed in turn affects the waves and currents. In figure 7.1 an overview is given of the terminology of the beach profile. 

The beach profile can, roughly, be subdivided into the offshore zone (lower shoreface), nearshore zone (or upper shoreface, surfzone), and the beach.

- In the offshore zone, the dominant processes are caused by oscillatory motions generated by shoaling waves, tidal and wind setup currents. 
- The nearshore zone is defined by wave breaking from the most offshore location effected by wave breaking until the mean sea level, see figure 7.1. This area is associated with a relative steep slope, often with several perturbations called bars and troughs. The dominant processes in this area are wave breaking, surf bores, mean flows (associated with wave set up), rips and longshore currents. 
- Lastly, the beach extends from mean sea level to the landward limit of wave and tidal influence.

## 7.1.1 Nearshore Zone Profile (Equilibrium Profile)
The nearshore zone is a hydrodynamically energetic area where sediment is transported by waves. As waves change from day to day, this zone is constantly evolving. The majority of beach change can be considered cyclic on the timescale of years, seasons or on tidal timescales.

The equilibrium profile is a beach cross-section that can be considered average on long timescales, even though it is unlikely to find the beach in the state at any particular time.

## 7.1.2 Bars and Troughs in the Nearshore Environment
The equilibrium profile assumes that the nearshore zone has a smooth profile. However, in reality major perturbation can be found, which are called bars and troughs. Bars and troughs in the nearshore environment are mainly associated with wave breaking. However, there are many other processes that influence bar and trough formation. Wave tank experiments suggest that bars and troughs are generated by sand moving offshore, excavated by plunging breakers. Often a series of different bar and trough systems are visible, each one associated with different wave conditions. High wave conditions generally move bars offshore, and milder wave conditions can cause bars to migrate back onshore.

Since heavy storms mainly occur during winter, bars and troughs often show seasonal movements. Large quantities of sand are removed at the troughs and deposit in bars far offshore during the winter. During the summer, sand is moved shoreward again. Often bars and troughs are well defined if the tidal range is small. This is because the breaking zone remains roughly at the same location.

# 7.2 Longshore Sediment Transport
Seasonal and storm-driven beach changes can cause significant (short-term) erosion through the adjustment of the cross-shore profile. However, on decadal timescales, the majority of beach erosion is often caused by plan-view, or along-beach, change driven by alongshore currents. This current is predominantly a result of waves breaking at an angle towards the shoreline and their subsequent release of radiation stresses due to wave breaking. However, a longshore current can be the combined effect of waves, tides and wind induced currents, and the resulting sediment transport is a complex three-dimensional transport mechanism.

Alongshore sediment transport is difficult to (re) create in a laboratory, and no theoretical predictions exist. Meaningful formulae that exist today are therefore empirical and only valid on relatively long timescales, integrated and time averaged over a period much longer than one wave period (typically months or years). This leads to the concepts of gross and net sediment transport. The gross transport is the amount of sediment that is transported without respect to the direction of movement within a certain period. Opposed to the gross transport the net transport does account for the direction of the sediment transport.

>[!Example]
>For example, 10 m$^3$ of transport north along the beach and subsequently 10 m$^3$ south along the beach results in 20 m$^3$ of gross alongshore sediment transport and 0 m$^3$ of net alongshore sediment transport

# 7.4 Alongshore Sediment Transport by Waves Creates Cuspate Deltas
Wave-dominated deltas, such as the Nile Delta or the Grijalva Delta (figure 7.7), are deltas where waves are the dominant factor shaping the fluvial sediment. Compared to tidal and river-dominated deltas, wave dominated deltas often have smooth coastlines, and few (≈ 1) distributary channels. The cuspate shape of wave-dominated deltas (figure 7.7) originates from the dynamics of alongshore sediment transport. In this section the mechanics of the dominant processes responsible for generating these cuspate shapes will be discussed

![[Pasted image 20241123150947.png]]

A river delta with a straight shoreline initially grows rapidly in offshore direction, see figure 7.8. Rapid growth is possible because the angle is not favorable for longshore sediment transport. Therefore, most sediment supplied by the river accumulates near the river mouth. However, as a result of this initial rapid growth, near the river mouth the angle becomes more favorable for longshore sediment transport. Sediment supplied by the river is carried away by the longshore current from the river mouth. Further away from the river mouth the angle decreases, resulting in a decrease of the longshore sediment transport. This (diffusive) process is responsible for the delta growth in lateral direction. Eventually, an equilibrium situation is achieved in which the delta grows, but its shape does not change significantly anymore (figure 7.8). This situation is characterized by a shoreline deflection such that sediment transport away from the river mouth (by alongshore sediment transport) is balanced by sediment brought towards the beach by the river mouth.
