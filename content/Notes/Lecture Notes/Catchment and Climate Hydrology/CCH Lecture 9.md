# Snow depth and snow water equivalent (SWE)
We often measure snow depth: easy observation. But snow-water equivalent needed for water balance modeling. This is the water contained as snow, which does not have a lot to do with snow depth, as snow density can vary.
- Requires weighing snow, which is very labour-intensive

When we talk about snow, we often talk about snow-water equivalent, not snow depth

Snow line is altitude above which snow covers the ground throughout the year. It varies strongly with latitude, and depends also on climate. 

Snow has an obvious impact on runoff, significantly delaying it. So most hydrological models have a snow module. 

Rain on snow is problematic, because rain has energy which can accelerate snowmelt. This can potentially double your runoff.

Some precipitation falling as snow can also decrease rainfall peaks, preventing further flooding.

Thus snow can both help and hinder

# Snow in models
In models, precipitation divided into rain and snow based on temperature threshold. Transition is often gradual and depends on atmospheric conditions. E.g. above a maximum temperature of 2-3 degrees no snow is possible. Below -0.5 - 0.5 degrees all precipitation typically falls as snow. In between, the fraction of snow is often assumed to vary linearly. 

Once snow has fallen, most models use degree-day concept. It is linearly and easy to work with, but its not purely  physical. Assumes all snow melt is because of snow melt exceeding 0 degrees. Any further exceedance than that has a linear effect. E.g. 7 days of 6 degrees gives 7x6 is 42 degree days
